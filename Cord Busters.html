Sky Finally Cuts The Lag On Every Sky Sports Channel

Live sport on Sky Glass and Sky Stream just got a little closer to actually being live. Sky has expanded Real Time, its feature that cuts the delay between something happening on the pitch and it appearing on your TV, to every Sky Sports channel.

If you’ve ever heard the neighbours cheer a goal half a minute before you saw it, you already know the problem.

Watching live TV over broadband runs behind a satellite dish or an aerial, and Real Time is Sky’s fix. Until now, though, it only worked on a handful of channels.

Since November 5, it covers 14 channels. Cricket, golf, F1, tennis and NFL have joined the football, and Main Event and F1 also get Ultra HD (4K) versions.

It’s a promise Sky first made back in 2024, when it cut the delay on a single channel and said more would follow “later this year”. It took a bit longer than that, and if you’re on Sky Q or NOW, you’re still waiting.

The Problem: Hearing The Goal Before You See It

Sky Glass (a TV with Sky built in) and Sky Stream (a small box that plugs into any TV) don’t use a satellite dish. Everything, including live channels, comes over your broadband. That’s fine for most TV, but it’s a problem for live sport.

Someone watching through a dish or an aerial typically sees a goal about 8-10 seconds after it happens. On a stream, you can be 30 to 40 seconds behind.

That’s plenty of time for a goal alert to pop up on your phone, or for your WhatsApp group to go mental, all while the move is still being built up on your screen.

The delay comes from buffering. A stream holds a chunk of video in reserve so it can keep playing smoothly when your connection wobbles, and that reserve is exactly what puts you behind.

Real Time trims that buffer so you’re closer to the live action. Sky’s announcement promises “less chance of hearing someone celebrate that big goal before you’ve seen it yourself” – though it still won’t put a number on how many seconds you’ll save.

There are two ways in. You can head straight to those channel numbers in the guide, or tune to the regular Sky Sports channel and press the “Watch in Real Time” button that appears on screen.

If you already used Real Time for football this season, note that one number has moved: Sky Sports+ was on 925 in August, and it’s now on 924, with cricket taking its old spot.

For F1 fans, lights out is exactly the kind of moment you don’t want to hear about from someone else first.

A Promise From 2024

Back in April 2024, Sky announced a low-latency mode for Sky Sports Main Event as part of a Sky Glass and Stream software update. Sky said it cut the delay by over 20 seconds, and that more Sky Sports channels would get it later that year.

The next big step didn’t come until June 2026, just before the World Cup. Sky rebranded the feature as Real Time and, in a slightly odd twist, switched it on for the BBC and ITV’s World Cup coverage. That meant those free-to-air matches could reach a Sky Glass or Sky Stream faster than they would through iPlayer or ITVX themselves.

At the time, we said it would almost certainly broaden. In August, it did, with Real Time arriving on Sky Sports Premier League, Football and Sky Sports+ for the start of the football season, alongside Sky’s new Multiview feature.

Now, finally, it covers everything. “Later this year” turned out to mean 2026, but at least Sky got there.

The Catches

First, it’s for Sky Glass and Sky Stream only. Sky Q customers don’t get it – which makes some sense, since Sky Q comes through a dish and is already in that 8-10 second range.

NOW customers miss out too, including those on the new Football and Multi-Sport memberships. NOW streams just like Glass and Stream do, so the lag is the same.

Sky hasn’t explained why NOW is left out, but Real Time leans on Sky controlling both the hardware and the software, and NOW runs as an app on other companies’ devices.

Second, it asks more of your broadband. When Real Time launched for the World Cup, Sky recommended 40Mbps, against 25Mbps for standard HD and 30Mbps for Ultra HD. With less of a buffer to fall back on, a shaky connection is more likely to stutter. There’s no word yet on whether the 4K Real Time channels need even more than that.

Third, at launch, you couldn’t rewind live TV with Real Time switched on. So if you wanted to watch a goal again, you had to come out of Real Time first. Sky hasn’t said that’s changed, so it’s fair to assume it still applies.

None of those are deal-breakers. If your broadband is decent and you mostly watch sport live, it’s an easy switch to make.

Sky Is Ahead Of iPlayer And Freely

The BBC has been trialling low latency on iPlayer since 2025, but it has to make it work on hundreds of different TVs and streaming sticks it doesn’t control, and so far it has been cautious about switching it on for everyone.

Freely, the streaming service from the people behind Freeview, carries the same built-in delay as any other stream, and nobody has announced a low-latency fix for it yet. During the World Cup, that delay was one of the downsides of Freely’s record-breaking tournament.

Sky, by contrast, makes the Glass TV and the Stream box, and writes the software that runs on them. That control over the whole chain is what lets it squeeze the delay down, and it’s now doing it on every sports channel it owns.

What It Means For Sky Q Holdouts

Sky’s streaming products are very clearly its future. In December 2025, Sky Q disappeared from Sky’s website, and new customers can now only get it by phone or in a shop.

Sky has extended its satellite deal “into the next decade”, but at some point, the millions of homes still on Sky Q will need to move to streaming.

For sports fans, lag has been one of the better arguments for keeping the dish. Real Time doesn’t fully match satellite (Sky doesn’t claim it does), but it should close a lot of the gap, and now on every Sky Sports channel.

Sky is going after the other big reason, recording, too. Sky OS 2.0 brings a Record button to Glass and Stream, with cloud recordings, and Sky expects it to reach everyone before the end of the year.
