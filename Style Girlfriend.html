People who were called gifted as children sometimes develop these 7 habits that quietly undermine their success as adults

Third grade. They finish the worksheet before the teacher is done handing it out, and she gives them the look. Not annoyed. Impressed, actually. You’re so smart. They hear it again from a parent at dinner, from an aunt at a birthday, from the test that pulled them into a different classroom on Thursdays.

Twenty years later they’re a week into their first real job, the chapter every guide to your twenties is built around, and they haven’t asked a single question. Everyone else has. They’re already behind, and nobody knows it yet.

Being called gifted teaches a kid one lesson above everything else: ease is the proof. If it came easy, you’re the real thing. If you had to work for it, maybe you weren’t.

The adult who grew out of that kid will pay almost any price to keep looking like it came easy, and the price is usually the thing they wanted.

1. They don’t practice anything they’re not already good at

A friend talks them into a pottery class. For forty minutes their bowl keeps collapsing, the instructor comes over twice, and the person at the next wheel is somehow fine. They never go back.

Practice was for the kids who needed it. They were told, repeatedly and by adults they trusted, that they didn’t. So anything that requires practice reads as evidence, and the evidence points the wrong way.

Social psychologist Natalie Kerr at James Madison University, writing about praise and mindset, describes a classic study in which fifth graders were praised for being smart after a set of problems. When the next set got hard, those kids lost interest, and they did worse on an easier set that came after.

One hard round was enough to undo them. The kids praised for effort kept going.

That pattern doesn’t stay in fifth grade. It shows up at the wheel, in the language app deleted after day three, in the guitar still in the closet. They’ll try almost anything once. Twice means they weren’t good at it, and they’d rather not know.

2. They walk in cold and call it confidence

It’s the night before a big interview and they’re on the couch rewatching a show they’ve already seen. The company’s website is open on their phone, unread. A friend texts: You ready? They send back a thumbs up.

Preparation would mean admitting they needed it, and they’ve gotten away with not needing it exactly often enough to keep doing it. Every list of interview tips for job seekers says the same things: do your homework, know the company, bring questions. They’ve read those lists. They assume those lists are for other people.

The interview goes fine. They’re sharp in the room, they improvise well, they get a second round. Then the second round asks about the company’s last product launch, and they’re guessing. The person across the table can tell.

What they can’t see is how much better fine could’ve been. Winging it caps them at good. Good was never the plan.

3. They’d rather figure it out than ask

Want me to walk you through it? a colleague asks on the first morning with the new system.

No, I’ve got it.

They don’t have it. Three hours later they’ve reverse-engineered most of what a five-minute walkthrough would have handed them, and they’ve got one piece wrong in a way they won’t find until next week.

Asking a question is the move a smart kid never had to make, so it feels like a confession. Every question is a small announcement that the thing didn’t come easy, and they’d rather spend three hours than make it.

The people who get ahead ask constantly. They ask in the first week, when nobody expects them to know anything and the question is free. This person waits until the question is expensive, then doesn’t ask it either.

4. They bury mistakes instead of fixing them

The report went out at four. At six, reading it again on the train, they see it: a number in the second table that’s off by a decimal. Nothing dramatic, easy to correct, the kind of error a quick note to the team would close out in a minute.

They don’t send the note. They open the shared file, change the number, and hope nobody compares versions.

The same study found that the kids praised for being smart were more likely to misreport their scores afterward than the kids praised for working hard.

Nobody told them to hide anything. They worked it out on their own. If you’re smart, you don’t make mistakes, so a mistake has to be made to disappear.

The edit holds for a while. Then a colleague references the old number in a meeting, and the small, fixable problem becomes a question about why the file changed and who changed it. A mistake owned in one sentence is forgettable. A mistake buried is a story.

5. They wait to be discovered

They’ve been about to apply for the senior role for two years. The posting comes around every spring. They read it, think that’s basically what I already do, and close the tab.

Applying means asking. Being asked means they were right. Somewhere in there is the belief that the right people will notice on their own, the way the teacher noticed, the way the test noticed. So they do the work, do it well, and wait.

This year a louder coworker applied and got it. Not a better one. Just louder.

6. They hear every correction as a demotion

Small thing, but next time, loop in finance before you send the deck.

Their manager says it in passing, on the way to another meeting, with a smile. It’s the lightest possible version of feedback, the kind of note most people would forget by lunch.

They don’t forget it. By the afternoon the sentence has turned into a question about whether they were ever as good as everyone said. By the end of the week they’ve over-delivered on three things nobody asked for and still haven’t looped in finance, because the correction became about them instead of the deck.

The manager was kind and the note was small, and the smallness is the part they can’t get past. A harsh critic they could dismiss. A gentle one is harder to argue with, so the gentleness goes straight through.

7. They’re still waiting for the real thing to start

Three jobs in six years. Two side projects, both at about sixty percent. A certification started and set aside. Meanwhile the friends from school have settled into unglamorous work, stayed, and gotten good at it. From the outside it reads as restlessness.

Psychotherapist Imi Lo, writing about gifted adults at work, notes that they often find it hard to settle on one career and are more likely to change paths several times. Fear of failing at the thing they were supposed to be good at can push them to drop pursuits they once loved.

Committing to one ordinary path means the label was wrong. If the real thing hasn’t started yet, the label is still true.

The friends with the unglamorous jobs got promoted this year. This person is updating a resume again, waiting for the version of their life that finally matches what they were told about it.
